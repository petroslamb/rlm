# Technical Blog Post Evaluation Rubric

This rubric defines the evaluation criteria for technical blog posts that achieve A+++ quality. Posts are evaluated on ten axes. Each axis is scored from 1 to 10. A score of 8+ on all axes constitutes A+++ quality.

## Evaluation Axes

### 1. Grounding in Research and Code

The post makes claims that are backed by citations, empirical results, or code artifacts. Assertions about performance, behavior, or capability reference specific papers, benchmarks, or implementation details. The reader can verify claims by following references or examining code. Speculation is clearly marked as such. The author does not make claims that cannot be substantiated.

A score of 10 means every significant claim is grounded. A score of 5 means claims are plausible but unverified. A score of 1 means the post makes unsubstantiated assertions.

### 2. Auditor-Explainer Voice

The author writes as someone who has examined the subject deeply and is now explaining what they found. The tone is that of a technical auditor presenting findings to informed peers. There is no marketing language, no hype, no superlatives without evidence. The author acknowledges limitations, trade-offs, and boundaries. Enthusiasm is expressed through depth of engagement rather than adjectives.

A score of 10 means the voice is consistently rigorous and honest. A score of 5 means the voice occasionally slips into promotional language. A score of 1 means the post reads like marketing copy.

### 3. Audience Calibration

The post is written for informed engineers, researchers, CTOs, and technical enthusiasts. It does not explain basic concepts. It assumes the reader understands programming, system design, and machine learning fundamentals. Technical terms are used without apology. The reader is treated as a peer who can evaluate claims critically.

A score of 10 means the post is appropriately dense for the target audience. A score of 5 means the post occasionally over-explains or under-explains. A score of 1 means the post talks down to the reader or assumes expertise they cannot have.

### 4. Value Density

Every paragraph advances the reader's understanding. There is no filler, no repetition, no throat-clearing. Introductions set context efficiently. Conclusions do not repeat what was said. Examples are chosen because they illuminate, not because the author had them available. The reader learns something concrete every few sentences.

A score of 10 means every paragraph is load-bearing. A score of 5 means the post could be shortened by 20% without losing content. A score of 1 means the post is padded with filler.

### 5. Prose Style

The text is dense prose. Paragraphs contain multiple sentences. There are no single-line paragraphs except for deliberate emphasis. There are no bulleted lists or numbered lists in the main body. There are no em dashes, no rhetorical flourishes that signal LLM-generated text. The writing is direct, concrete, and efficient.

A score of 10 means the prose style is consistent throughout. A score of 5 means occasional stylistic lapses (stray bullets, single-line paragraphs). A score of 1 means the post violates prose style in every section.

### 6. Examples in Code

Concepts are illustrated with executable code examples. Examples are minimal and focused. They show exactly what the concept means in practice without extraneous setup. Example outputs are realistic and match what the code would produce. The reader can copy examples into their own environment and expect them to work.

A score of 10 means every significant concept has a code example. A score of 5 means examples are present but incomplete or unclear. A score of 1 means examples are missing or broken.

### 7. Technical Accuracy

The post does not contain technical errors. Descriptions of systems, APIs, and behaviors match their actual implementation. Code examples are syntactically correct. Claims about performance or capability reflect real measurements. The author has tested what they describe.

A score of 10 means the post is technically flawless. A score of 5 means minor inaccuracies that do not affect the main argument. A score of 1 means significant technical errors that undermine credibility.

### 8. Structure and Flow

The post has a logical structure that guides the reader through the argument. Each section builds on the previous. The reader is never confused about why they are reading a given paragraph. Transitions between sections are smooth. The structure serves the content rather than forcing content into a template.

A score of 10 means the structure is elegant and serves the argument. A score of 5 means the structure is functional but mechanical. A score of 1 means the reader gets lost or confused about the argument's direction.

### 9. Limitations and Trade-offs

The post explicitly states what the solution does not do. Limitations are not hidden in footnotes or mentioned in passing. Trade-offs are presented as engineering decisions rather than apologized for. The reader finishes the post with a clear understanding of when to use the solution and when not to.

A score of 10 means limitations are prominently and honestly discussed. A score of 5 means limitations are mentioned but downplayed. A score of 1 means the post oversells and ignores obvious trade-offs.

### 10. Actionability

The reader can act on what they learned. There is a clear path from reading to doing. If the post describes software, there are installation instructions and a demo. If the post describes a concept, there are references for deeper learning. The reader is not left wondering what to do next.

A score of 10 means the post ends with clear next steps. A score of 5 means next steps are implied but not explicit. A score of 1 means the reader finishes with no idea how to proceed.

## Usage Notes

This rubric is designed for technical blog posts that present original work, explain research papers, or document engineering contributions. It is not appropriate for tutorials, news summaries, or opinion pieces, which have different goals and audiences.

When scoring, evaluate each axis independently before computing the total. A single low-scoring axis may indicate a structural problem that affects the entire post. Posts should be revised to bring all axes above threshold before publication.
